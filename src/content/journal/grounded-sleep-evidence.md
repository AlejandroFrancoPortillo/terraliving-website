Two framings dominate the grounding-sheet conversation, and both are useless.

The first is the dismissive one: *this is wellness pseudo-science, there's no mechanism, it's the marketing of an idea.* The second is the testimonial one: *I slept on a sheet and my life changed.* The first won't engage with what's measurable; the second won't engage with what's contested. Anyone trying to actually figure out whether grounded sleep is worth doing gets stranded between them.

This piece is neither. The mechanism is real and instrumentable. The evidence is also small, often run by people with a commercial interest in the outcome, and short of a large independent trial. Both things are true at the same time. Here's how to think about it.

## The mechanism in ninety seconds

A grounded sheet has a conductive grid — in our case, organic cotton woven with a 7% silver-fibre lattice — connected via a cord to the ground prong of a properly earthed wall outlet. A 98 kΩ safety resistor sits inline. The resistor is the entire safety architecture; it caps current to a fraction of a microampere, well below any sensation threshold, well below any electrical risk.

When your bare skin touches the conductive grid, electrons move continuously between your body and the earth's surface charge. You can verify this with a standard multimeter: put the probes between skin and ground, and you'll measure a small but real voltage equalization. This isn't disputed. The mechanism is electron transfer, and it's instrumentable.

What is disputed is what that electron transfer does physiologically. That's where the evidence gets thinner.

## The four studies that matter

There are dozens of grounding papers in the literature now. Most are small. Most come from a relatively small cluster of researchers with financial relationships to grounding products. The four that anchor the field — the ones we'd point to honestly — are these:

### Ghaly & Teplitz (2004) — circadian cortisol over 8 weeks

Twelve subjects with complaints of sleep dysfunction, pain, and stress were grounded during sleep in their own beds for 8 weeks using a conductive mattress pad.[^1] Saliva samples taken at 4-hour intervals over a 24-hour period established each subject's circadian cortisol profile at baseline.

After 8 weeks, the cortisol secretion patterns shifted: night-time cortisol levels dropped, and the curve resynchronised more closely to the natural diurnal rhythm (lower at night, higher in the morning). Subjects also reported improvements in falling asleep, sleep quality, and waking rested.

The study has all the obvious limitations — small sample, no control group, self-report subjective outcomes — but the cortisol shift was measured by saliva assay, not self-report. That part is harder to wave away.

### Chevalier & Sinatra (2011) — heart rate variability and autonomic tone

Twenty-seven subjects, double-blind protocol.[^2] HRV was measured during a 2-hour grounding session versus a sham-grounded control. Grounded subjects showed measurable increases in parasympathetic activity (RMSSD, HF power) and decreases in sympathetic dominance (LF/HF ratio).

HRV is a more rigorous physiological signal than self-reported sleep quality. The change was small in absolute terms — but it was directional, repeatable across subjects, and consistent with what you'd predict if continuous low-current electron transfer was nudging the autonomic nervous system toward parasympathetic dominance.

### Sokal & Sokal (2011) — physiology series

Karol and Pawel Sokal, physicians in Poland independently of the US grounding-research cluster, ran a series of small experiments on continuous overnight grounding.[^3] They documented changes in calcium-ion and inorganic phosphorus serum levels, renal excretion patterns, electroencephalography (EEG), and somatosensory evoked potentials.

What makes the Sokal series interesting isn't the size — none of their experiments enrolled more than a few dozen subjects — but the independence. The authors aren't part of the Chevalier / Sinatra / Earthing Institute commercial network. They saw measurable physiological changes in the same direction as the US group, which is what you'd hope for if a real mechanism is present rather than wishful interpretation of noisy data.

### Chevalier et al. (2012) — narrative review

The narrative review that consolidates the mechanism case and surveys the small-trial evidence base.[^4] It's the place to start if you want one document that lays out what's claimed and the supporting studies. It's also the document to read with awareness that the lead authors are commercially interested in the field.

## Where the studies agree, and where they don't

The studies agree, broadly:
- The mechanism — electron transfer through bare skin to a properly earthed conductor — is real and verifiable.
- A subset of physiological markers (cortisol rhythm, HRV, certain inflammation markers) shift in a direction consistent with parasympathetic / anti-inflammatory effects when subjects sleep grounded over weeks.
- Self-report sleep quality consistently improves in subjects who try it. (Easy to dismiss as placebo. Also: that's actually how sleep is experienced.)

The studies do not yet agree, or do not yet show:
- The magnitude of the effect across a general healthy population. Most studies enrol subjects with existing complaints (poor sleep, chronic stress, pain). The healthy-baseline effect size is less well characterised.
- Whether the effect generalises to short partial-night exposure (a few hours) versus full-night continuous overnight grounding over weeks.
- The mechanism in deeper detail — whether the dominant effect is via electron-driven antioxidant action, direct ionic shifts, or vagal-tone modulation downstream of either.

## The honest limitation

There is no large independent randomised controlled trial of grounded sleep against a properly sham-controlled bed. There should be one. The field has been making that case to itself for fifteen years and still doesn't have it.

This is the single biggest reason to be honest rather than enthusiastic about the evidence. Small studies in selected populations, often run by financially-interested researchers, produce signals that can survive into meta-review without ever being adequately stress-tested by independent labs. The grounding literature is in that zone. Better than nothing; less than convincing.

## What we cannot claim

The Earthsheet is not a medical device. We have not registered it with the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration. We make no claims that it treats, prevents, or cures any condition. Specifically: we are not selling it as treatment for insomnia, jet lag, anxiety, chronic pain, inflammation, or any other condition with a medical name. The evidence base doesn't support those claims at the level the TGA would require, and we wouldn't make them even if it did.

What we do say: there's a real mechanism, a small evidence base showing physiological signals in a particular direction, a long subjective-report tradition, and our own experience that grounded sleep changed our own subjective sense of how we rest. None of that is a therapeutic claim. All of it is true.

## What would update our view

A few specific results we're watching for:

- **A large independent RCT** — properly powered (n > 200), pre-registered, sham-controlled, run by a research group with no financial relationship to grounding products. If the cortisol and HRV shifts seen in the small studies don't replicate at scale, we'd want to know, and we'd write about it here.
- **Inflammation biomarker resolution** — the inflammation papers in the Chevalier corpus rely on subjective inflammation scoring and a few biomarkers. A clean CRP / IL-6 / TNF-α study with continuous grounding would be more persuasive than anything that currently exists.
- **A null result we can't explain** — if a well-designed study comes back with no measurable shift in autonomic or endocrine markers, we'd take it seriously. Not as proof of nothing happening, but as a signal that the effect is smaller than the existing literature suggests.

## Closing — the slowest-changing input you have

Your bed is the input you spend the most time touching. Roughly a third of your life. Anything that nudges parasympathetic tone during sleep compounds across decades.

The grounding sheet isn't an intervention you feel. It isn't a drug, isn't a stimulant, doesn't have a sensation. It works (if it works) by accumulation — small autonomic shifts, night after night, over months. The kind of input the franco-engage frame would call a substrate, not a hack.

If you're someone who wants to feel something dramatic from a single night, this isn't that product. If you're someone who already understands that the daily inputs compound — the food, the morning light, the cold, the breath — and is looking for the night-time addition to that set, this is what it is.

We can't promise you anything. We can promise you the mechanism is real, the studies are honest about their limits, and we will tell you if the evidence moves.
