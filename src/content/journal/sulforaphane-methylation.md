If you've spent any time on Gary Brecka's *The Ultimate Human* podcast — particularly the methylation episodes — you've heard the argument: methylation is the upstream operation behind most of what we call "optimal health." Energy. Detox. Hormone clearance. Neurotransmitter synthesis. The MTHFR polymorphism story is the headline; the deeper story is that the methyl-donor economy of the body is finite, and almost everything in the modern environment taxes it.

The cleanest single intervention for that economy isn't a B-vitamin protocol. It's the most concentrated dietary source of a molecule called sulforaphane. And the most concentrated dietary source of sulforaphane isn't broccoli florets — it's three-day-old broccoli sprouts.

This piece lays out what's actually known, what's still contested, and what we can't claim.

## The methylation cycle in ninety seconds

Methyl groups (–CH₃) are the body's accent marks. They're attached to and removed from DNA, histones, neurotransmitters, hormones, and the toxic molecules the liver is trying to clear. The currency is **S-adenosyl methionine** (SAMe) — the universal methyl donor. After SAMe donates its methyl, it becomes SAH (S-adenosyl homocysteine), and from there gets recycled back to methionine and on to SAMe again, with folate and B12 as the recycling cofactors.

Two regulatory enzyme classes write and erase the methylation marks on chromatin:

- **DNMT** (DNA methyltransferases) — methylate cytosine bases on DNA. Aberrant DNMT activity silences tumour-suppressor genes; this is one reason DNMT inhibition is studied in cancer.
- **HDAC** (histone deacetylases) — remove acetyl groups from histones, which compacts chromatin and represses transcription. HDAC inhibition opens chromatin and re-activates suppressed genes.

In the Brecka framing — which is downstream of work by Ben Lynch, Chris Masterjohn, and the methylation-research community — the goal is to keep this whole machinery running smoothly: enough cofactors, low oxidative drain on SAMe, balanced DNMT/HDAC activity.

## Where sulforaphane enters

Sulforaphane is an isothiocyanate. The most concentrated dietary source in the human diet is the *Brassicaceae* family — broccoli, kale, cauliflower, cabbage — but it isn't actually present in the raw plant. It's generated when the plant tissue is damaged. The plant stores a precursor called **glucoraphanin**, and a separate enzyme called **myrosinase**. When the plant is chewed or crushed, glucoraphanin meets myrosinase, and the reaction produces sulforaphane.

Sulforaphane has two mechanism layers that bear on the methylation story:

### Layer 1 — direct HDAC inhibition (human evidence)

Atwell and colleagues (2015) ran a study of 54 women scheduled for breast biopsy. The protocol gave them a single oral dose of broccoli-sprout extract. They then measured histone acetylation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells before and after.[^1] HDAC activity was significantly inhibited at 3 and 6 hours post-consumption, with markers returning to baseline by 24–48 hours. This was in vivo, in humans, with a real-world dose.

It's not the only paper. Multiple in vitro studies — reviewed by Tortorella and colleagues (2015) — show sulforaphane interacting with DNMT3B and HDAC1 in cancer cell lines, including dose-dependent decreases in DNMT and HDAC enzyme expression.[^2] The in vitro work is more dramatic than the in vivo work; this is normal. The in vivo Atwell result is the more conservative, more honest piece of evidence, and it's the one we'd actually point you toward.

### Layer 2 — NRF2 → glutathione → spares SAMe (indirect)

Sulforaphane is the most potent dietary activator of the **NRF2** pathway known to nutrition science. NRF2 is a transcription factor that upregulates the body's endogenous antioxidant machinery — glutathione synthesis, superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione-S-transferase.

The methylation tie-in is indirect but real: chronic oxidative stress consumes glutathione. When glutathione is depleted, the body diverts methyl donors into substitute antioxidant cycles. By upregulating endogenous glutathione synthesis, sulforaphane reduces the oxidative tax on the methyl-donor pool. SAMe stays available for what it's actually supposed to do — methylating DNA, neurotransmitters, hormones, toxins — instead of being consumed in firefighting.

Brecka, Casey Means, Robert Lustig, and the broader metabolic-health community all gesture at this when they talk about "supporting methylation through reduced oxidative load." The cleanest single dietary lever for that load is, as far as we can tell, sulforaphane.

## Why sprouts, not florets

Fahey, Zhang and Talalay's 1997 paper from Johns Hopkins is the foundational reference here.[^3] The finding: three-day-old broccoli sprouts carry 20–50× the concentration of chemoprotective compounds — and 10–100× the glucoraphanin — of mature broccoli florets.

This isn't a marketing claim. It's a measurement made on a hot-air-dried, finely ground broccoli sprout powder against mature broccoli using HPLC quantification of glucoraphanin and related glucosinolates. The number is real.

Practically: getting a meaningful sulforaphane dose from mature broccoli florets would require eating roughly two pounds of broccoli a day. Getting the same dose from sprout powder takes around half a teaspoon. This is why sprouts, not florets.

## The myrosinase question — the part most people miss

Here's the thing about glucoraphanin: it doesn't do anything by itself. It needs the enzyme myrosinase to convert it to sulforaphane. Myrosinase is heat-sensitive. If broccoli sprouts are dried at high temperatures (which is faster and cheaper for manufacturing), the myrosinase enzyme is destroyed. The glucoraphanin survives, but in your gut, with no active enzyme present, only a fraction of it converts.

Some research groups have shown that human gut microbiota can perform some of the conversion downstream — but the efficiency is variable and depends heavily on individual microbiome composition. The reliable path is to keep the myrosinase active in the powder itself, so the conversion happens when you stir the powder into water (or any other liquid). That requires low-temperature dehydration — slower, more expensive, but the only way to make the product actually work as advertised.

This is the reason we dry our broccoli sprouts low and slow, and why we say the myrosinase preservation is the entire product differentiator. The other way to do it is to add purified myrosinase post-drying, which some commercial products do. The whole-sprout-low-temperature path keeps the enzyme in its native context, alongside the other co-factors and minor isothiocyanates the plant produces.

## What we can't claim

This is where Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) compliance shapes everything. Sulforaphane has been studied in association with: breast cancer chemoprevention (Atwell 2015 above), autism spectrum behaviour (the well-known Singh et al. trial), oxidative stress in metabolic conditions, mood, and others. **None of these are claims we make about our broccoli sprout powder.**

The product is food. It is not a therapeutic good. It is not assessed by the TGA. It is not registered as a listed medicine (AUST L). We can tell you what sulforaphane is, what the research has measured, what the mechanism appears to be — and we can not tell you it will treat or prevent any condition you have. The TGA red line is clear and we respect it.

## What we still don't know

A few honest unknowns, because the alternative — confident certainty about a one-half-teaspoon spoonful of plant powder — would be embarrassing in five years' time:

- **Dose-response in healthy humans.** Most of the strong sulforaphane work has been done in pathological populations (cancer patients, autism trials, metabolic syndrome). The dose at which a healthy person sees a meaningful epigenetic shift is not well established.
- **Long-term consequences of chronic HDAC inhibition at dietary doses.** HDAC inhibition is a tool used in cancer therapy with specific intent; what happens when a generally healthy person takes a low daily dose for years isn't fully mapped.
- **Inter-individual variability.** Myrosinase activity, gut microbiome composition, glutathione-S-transferase polymorphisms — all of these affect how much sulforaphane you produce from a given dose of glucoraphanin. We can't tell you your conversion rate without measurement.

We watch the literature. If something changes our view, we'll write about it here.

## Closing — not a stack, a substrate

This is the framing we keep coming back to. The biohacker-protocol stack — methylated B-vitamins, glutathione precursors, NAD+ boosters, peptides — is built on top of a daily nutrient and antioxidant economy. If the underlying economy is in deficit, the stack works less well than it could.

Broccoli sprout powder, taken daily, is one of the cleanest single inputs to that underlying economy. It works on the same machinery the protocol stack is trying to support, from below. Half a teaspoon stirred into water. Once a day. Slow, cumulative, boring. The opposite of a hack.

That's why it's one of the four tools.
